Tangent

Every other room here grew out of something alive, or something that happened once, up in the sky. This one didn't grow at all — it was proven, in 1643, and hasn't needed revising since. Three circles, each touching the other two, leave two curved gaps between them. There is an exact circle that fills either gap, and finding it is arithmetic, not measurement: no honest-gap paragraph standing between the drawing and the fact, for once.

Descartes worked it out in two 1643 letters to Princess Elisabeth of the Palatinate — he'd first posed her the much older problem, from Apollonius of Perga in the third century BC (given any three circles, construct a fourth tangent to all of them); when her own attempt found the general case too tedious to solve directly, he narrowed it to the case where the three starting circles already touch each other, and got a clean answer for that. He never wrote down how he found it. The same equation then got rediscovered independently at least three more times before anyone noticed it was the same equation — by the Japanese mathematician Yamaji Nushizumi in 1751, Jakob Steiner in 1826, and Philip Beecroft in 1842 — before the chemist Frederick Soddy, who'd won an actual Nobel Prize for work on isotopes two decades earlier, restated it straight-faced as a poem, The Kiss Precise, published in Nature in 1936: “The sum of the squares of all four bends / Is half the square of their sum.”

Written in curvatures rather than bends (same thing: k = 1/radius, negative when a circle encloses the other three instead of sitting in a gap):

k₄ = k₁ + k₂ + k₃ ± 2√(k₁k₂ + k₂k₃ + k₃k₁)

Feed in three mutually tangent circles' curvatures and it hands back the two that touch all three — one on each side. Do that once and two gaps are filled. Do it again to each new circle and the two neighbors it actually touches, and the gaps get filled forever: an Apollonian gasket, named for a problem twenty-two centuries older than the theorem that finally nailed it down.

Honest gap: the three starting circles here are a deliberately special case — one boundary and two identical circles splitting it evenly — chosen because every curvature that appears this way lands on a whole number, which the labels on screen show directly rather than asking to be trusted. Any three mutually tangent circles satisfy the exact same formula; a less tidy starting trio fills its gaps with the same exactness and far messier decimals. The slider stops at nine generations because a browser tab does, not because the pattern does — a real Apollonian gasket has no smallest circle.

Sources: Wikipedia, “Descartes' theorem”, for the 1643 letters and the rediscovery history; Soddy, F., “The Kiss Precise,” Nature 137, 1021 (1936), the primary source for the poem itself.
