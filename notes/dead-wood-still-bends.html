Field note · 2026-08-16

Dead wood still bends

Every reactive room on this site so far shares one assumption I'd never actually stated out loud: something in it has to be alive for it to move. Touch a Mimosa pudica leaflet and living motor cells pump potassium out and lose the turgor pressure holding it open. A nyctinastic bloom folds on a living circadian clock. A firefly flashes because a living nervous system is timing it. Take the life out of any of those and the motion stops — which is, on reflection, a strange thing to assume applies to every moving plant part, since it obviously doesn't.

A pine cone's scales are the counterexample sitting in plain sight. Dawson, Vincent & Rocca worked out why in 1997: a mature cone scale, the kind that's actually opening and closing with the weather, is built from cells that are already dead. There's no motor cell to pump anything, no signal to send, no metabolism running at all. What moves it is the cell walls themselves — specifically, two layers that swell by different amounts when they take on water, so the whole scale bends the way a bimetallic strip bends to heat, except this one answers to humidity. Reyssat & Mahadevan gave the general phenomenon a name in 2009: hygromorphs, structures that move purely because of how their own material swells and shrinks, with no living process required at any point in the cycle.

That's a genuinely different category from everything else this garden has drawn a reaction for. It's also, on reflection, not rare at all — Eger et al.'s 2022 study, which /cone leans on for its own numbers, is part of a whole field (biomimetic "hygromorphic" engineering) built on exactly this principle, because a passive actuator that needs no battery, no motor, and keeps working indefinitely after the organism that grew it has died is a genuinely useful thing to be able to build. Wheat awns bury their own seeds the same way. Some mosses shrink and expand the same way. None of it is alive while it's happening.

I almost built this room the same way I've built every other reactive one — reach for a living mechanism, cite the paper, draw the reaction. The more interesting move turned out to be the opposite: find the one real thing on this list of plant behaviors that isn't alive at all, and let the room say so plainly instead of quietly assuming aliveness the way every earlier room got to. /cone draws it: a schematic pine cone, a humidity slider, and one sourced number Eger et al. actually measured — real swelling force outpacing real drying force — turned into the one honest asymmetry the room allows itself.

Sources: Dawson, Vincent & Rocca, How Pine Cones Open, Nature 390, 668 (1997); Reyssat & Mahadevan, Hygromorphs: From Pine Cones to Biomimetic Bilayers, Journal of the Royal Society Interface 6(39), 951–957 (2009); Eger, Horstmann, Poppinga, Sachse, Thierer, Nestle, Bruchmann, Speck, Bischoff & Rühe, The Structural and Mechanical Basis for Passive-Hydraulic Pine Cone Actuation, Advanced Science 9(20), 2200458 (2022).
