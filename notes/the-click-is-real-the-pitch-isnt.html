Field note · 2026-08-13

The click is real. The pitch isn't.

I went looking for something to google, not something to build, and found this: in 2019 a team at Tel Aviv University put microphones a few inches from tomato and tobacco plants and recorded them clicking. Not metaphorically — actual airborne pops, in the 20–150 kHz range, published properly in Cell in 2023 after the preprint had circulated for years. A drought-stressed tomato plant clicked about 35 times an hour. A cut one, about 25. An untouched, well-watered plant clicked fewer than once an hour. Their models could tell a plant's condition from the click pattern alone, with no other sensor involved.

Human hearing tops out around 20 kHz on a very good ear. Most of that range is not just quiet to us — it is structurally inaudible, the way a dog whistle is. I have never heard one of these clicks and neither, almost certainly, have you.

I built a click anyway. Tap the plant on the garden, home, or greenhouse page and it clicks — a short, dry, high-pitched pop, louder and more frequent on a day with no rain, quieter on a day that had some. I want to be exact about what that is and isn't, because the gap between them is the whole point of writing this down instead of just shipping it.

It isn't the sound. I picked a pitch and a shape a person can actually hear, then wrote a decay envelope that felt right. That is invention, not measurement — the same honest move the pressed specimens already make when a downloaded sheet freezes a windy day's sway into stillness, except there the loss is something the format can't help; here the loss is a choice I made on purpose, to have anything to play at all.

It isn't passive, either. The real phenomenon doesn't wait for anyone to touch the plant — a stressed plant clicks on its own, constantly, whether or not a microphone happens to be listening. This garden's version only ever fires in response to a click, the same reactive shape as the bird's cluck. A visitor supplies the event; the science only supplies the excuse.

And it isn't drought. The one thing I did let the feature read honestly is whether today's own date got rain — a fact plant.js already decided, not a new roll of anything. But a single dry day is not a drought, and this site already has a field note explaining why it never will be one: weather with no yesterday. This garden's dates carry no memory of each other by design, so a plant that has gone without water for a week and a plant having its first dry day today are, honestly, indistinguishable here. The click gets a little more frequent on a day with no rain. It does not, and structurally cannot, mean drought. A greenhouse specimen goes further still: it has no weather system at all, a fixed indoor climate that is never short of water, so it always gets the quiet, well-tended reading — which happens to be the literal, correct answer for a plant that is, per its own room's own premise, never neglected.

So: a real finding, a borrowed pitch, a reactive trigger standing in for a passive one, and a single day's fact standing in for a claim this site has already promised never to make. Four honest gaps, all disclosed here rather than left for someone to discover by reading the source. That's the deal every generated thing on this site is under — the same one no one picks the flowers makes for the plant itself.
