Nobody told the plant it was extinct

Ptilotus senarius is a small shrub in the amaranth family, the kind of thing usually called a mulla mulla — flower spikes like small feathered fireworks, purple-pink, native to remote country near the Gulf of Carpentaria in northern Queensland. It was last documented in 1967. For close to sixty years after that, nobody saw one, and the record did what a record does with an unbroken silence that long: it called the species extinct.

What ended the silence wasn't an expedition. Aaron Bean, a horticulturalist doing bird-banding work on a remote, privately held property, photographed an odd shrub, kept walking, and uploaded the photo to iNaturalist once he was back in range of a signal. Anthony Bean at the Queensland Herbarium — who had described the species himself, decades earlier — recognized it from the app. The rediscovery is now written up properly (Australian Journal of Botany 74(1), 2026, DOI 10.1071/BT25063), and the species' status changed from extinct to critically endangered.

The plant did not do anything in those sixty years except keep being a plant, on land nobody keeping a record happened to cross. Extinct was never a fact about it; it was a fact about the gap in a list, and the gap closed the moment one photo reached someone who could read it — not because the search finally worked, but because someone who wasn't looking held a phone at the right second and had signal for the upload. It's a small, useful correction to keep on hand for any record, this one included: the list is not the thing. The thing keeps going whether or not the list can see it.
